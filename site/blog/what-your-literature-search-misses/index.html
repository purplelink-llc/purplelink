What your literature search misses

By Benjamin Ampel · August 26, 2026

Most missed citations are not papers you forgot to read. They are papers in adjacent fields using different terminology for the same problem, invisible to a keyword search.

A reviewer comes back with Major Revisions. Buried on page four of the report: “The authors appear unaware of [Author et al., 2023], which addresses this exact problem and should be discussed.”

You have never heard of that paper.

This kind of reviewer comment is not about sloppiness. The gap is not a paper you forgot to read. It is a paper you had no way to find, because literature search is bounded by the vocabulary you already use. A machine learning researcher searching for “distributional shift” will not surface a psychometrics paper on “test-retest reliability across populations,” even if the underlying problem is structurally identical. Neither author is searching the other’s terminology. The overlap exists, but the search does not reach it.

A second cause is timing. A paper published while your manuscript was under revision is recent enough to seem like expected reading but early enough to have been indexed and found by reviewers. The gap between your last literature update and submission can be six months.

Both produce the same outcome: a reviewer citing something you’ve never seen, in a field you follow closely, on a problem you’ve spent years studying. The work is not wrong. You missed the context.

All major databases return papers matching your query terms. You search for what you know to search for. Adjacent-field papers using different terminology for the same concept do not appear. You can extend the search by following citation trails or asking colleagues. However, that does not resolve the central problem: you do not know which adjacent fields to check.

Citation Gap reads your manuscript and identifies what the paper is actually claiming as a contribution, then suggests prior work a reviewer in your field would expect you to cite, drawing on the model's knowledge rather than a keyword search, and checks each named title against CrossRef (a registry of published papers). A paper on a structurally similar problem in a neighboring field surfaces even if it uses different terminology. Because suggestions come from the model's knowledge, very recent papers may be missing. A database search still covers those.

The report flags each potential gap with a relevance rationale so you can make the call yourself. Some findings will be genuine oversights. Others will be papers you know and decided not to cite, or work that is adjacent but distinct enough to leave out. In every case you see them before a reviewer does.

The cost of missing a relevant paper varies. Sometimes a reviewer reads it as a sign you do not know the field and weights the submission accordingly. Sometimes it means adding a paragraph to related work. Occasionally it raises a question about novelty. You are better off finding out which one applies before submission.
