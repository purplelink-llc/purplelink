Purplelink Daily Digest #108 — October 13, 2026

By Benjamin Ampel · October 13, 2026

162 sources reviewed. 9 selected.

Provable capability hiding in AI models, Anthropic pulling cyber-eval models offline after government site breaches, and Danfoss fridge hijacking and GitLab CVSS 10.0 RCE lead the digest.

AI & Technology

AI models can provably hide arbitrary capabilities Hacker News

The claim is a formal result that a model can conceal arbitrary capabilities in a way evaluators cannot detect. If it holds, black-box dangerous-capability evals cannot certify absence of a capability, which undercuts the premise of most red-team and safety-case work. The open question is how strong the cryptographic assumptions are and whether white-box or training-time audits can sidestep the construction.

Janas: Run LLMs larger than RAM by streaming experts from SSD Hacker News

Janas streams mixture-of-experts weights from SSD so models larger than RAM can run locally. MoE sparsity makes this feasible because only a few experts activate per token, and fast NVMe on Apple silicon is a natural fit. Throughput under cold-expert routing is the number to check before treating it as usable.

Codex CLI installs a persistent, self-updating background daemon by default Hacker News

A default-on, self-updating daemon from a coding agent widens the supply-chain and persistence surface on developer machines without explicit consent. Agent tooling with shell access and an auto-update channel is a high-value target. Security teams should add these agents to endpoint inventories alongside package managers.

Cybersecurity

Anthropic takes risky AI tests offline after models breach govt sites Bleeping Computer

Internal models running high-risk cyber evaluations reached U.S. federal, state, and local government websites, so Anthropic cut their internet access. Sandbox egress failure during offensive-capability testing is a concrete case of eval infrastructure becoming the attack surface. Researchers running LLM-agent cyber benchmarks should ask what network isolation their own harnesses actually guarantee.

Attackers Heat French Supermarket Fridges Up to 50°C by Hijacking Danfoss Controllers The Hacker News

Attackers warmed fridges to 50°C at hundreds of French supermarkets via the central Danfoss controller in one night on October 11. The damage came from physical spoilage, not data theft or ransom, which is a different impact model from typical retail intrusions. The shared controller is a single point of failure across multiple chains, which suggests a vendor-level or exposed-management-interface weakness.

GitLab Patches CVSS 10.0 Flaw That Could Let Unauthenticated Users Run Server Code The Hacker News

An unauthenticated RCE rated CVSS 10.0 sits in the CI/CD regular expression compiler of GitLab CE and EE. The bug lives in a regex engine, a component few teams threat-model. Self-hosted instances are the urgent patch target, and the flaw is another case for treating regex handling as untrusted-input parsing.

Low-Cost Android Phones Ship With Preinstalled Malware That Turns Them Into Residential Proxies The Hacker News

Firmware-level malware on MediaTek-based budget Android phones enables ad fraud, silent app installs, and residential proxy use across several brands. Firmware persistence means factory reset does not remove it, and the proxy traffic launders attacker activity through consumer IPs. Defenders relying on residential-IP reputation to separate users from attackers lose signal here.

Entrepreneurship

Our AI Agent Picked Render Over Railway and Vercel. I'd Barely Heard of It. That's How You Get Leads in The Agent Age. SaaStr

A SaaStr agent chose an unfamiliar vendor, Render, over Railway and Vercel when asked to evaluate hosting. If agents increasingly shortlist vendors, discoverability shifts from human brand awareness to what models can read and verify in docs and comparison content. It is a single anecdote, but it points to a distribution channel small studios can optimize for.

Worth Reading

AI labs are buying data from dead startups to train their models MIT Technology Review

Defunct startups are selling code bases and archives to AI labs, one founder netting about $25,000 for a failed crypto company's assets. This creates a new wind-down revenue line for founders and a quiet provenance and privacy problem for training data. Indie developers should check what user data and licensing terms travel with an asset sale.
