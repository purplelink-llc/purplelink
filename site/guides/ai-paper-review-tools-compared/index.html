Comparison, tested

The best AI paper reviewers in 2026, tested on one real manuscript

By Benjamin Ampel · September 26, 2026 · Updated November 13, 2026

I ran a public eLife manuscript through AI reviewers and scored each review against the 12 points eLife's human reviewers raised. A plain Claude chat did about as well as our own $39 Deep Review, and our tool made one confident mistake. This guide covers that result, which tool fits which job, and what each one costs.

Disclosure up front: Purplelink makes Paper Review, one of the tools compared here. I ran it the same way as the others and report where it did worse. Prices come from each vendor's own pages, read on November 13, 2026. Plans change, so check before you pay.

The short answer

On one psychology and neuroscience manuscript, the Paper Review Deep tier and a single plain prompt to Claude each raised all 12 of the eLife reviewers' points at least in part. Deep raised 5 in full and the Claude chat raised 6. Each also found real errors the human reviewers did not mention, and they found different ones. Deep recomputed the statistics and caught a one-tailed test reported as two-tailed. The chat caught a supplementary table whose numbers do not add up.

Deep also made a mistake. It marked as a "high confidence" contradiction a set of results that are really a mislabelled table column, and it suggested the numbers might come from different analysis runs. The chat read the same table correctly. So the honest result is this: on this paper, a $39 structured review was not clearly better than a careful chat prompt. Its advantages were recomputed statistics, a fixed report format and an annotated PDF, not more correct findings.

One manuscript is a small test. It does not rank these tools in general, and the tools I could not run yet are marked "not run" below rather than guessed at.

How I tested

The test paper is "Betrayal is worse than loss during cooperation" by Tang, Tan, Gao, Lin, Gan, Ding and Gao, a bioRxiv preprint released under CC BY 4.0. It has a behavioural task, computational models and EEG, so there is a lot for a reviewer to check. eLife reviewed it and published the reviews as reviewed preprint 111043. I condensed those reviews into 12 distinct points and used them as the answer key.

  • Paper Review, Deep tier. Run on November 13, 2026 with a single-use code, so no money changed hands. The list price is $39.
  • Plain Claude chat. Claude Sonnet 5.5, one plain prompt asking for a critical review, the paper's extracted text as input, no web search and no code tools.
  • Manusights free Readiness Scan. Run the same day. The paid Full Review was not bought.
  • Stanford's PaperReview.ai. Submitted the same day. It sends results by email and the result had not arrived when this was published.

Each review was graded against the 12 points: raised in full, partly raised, or missed. The grading was done by an AI grader and checked by me, and both of us knew which review was ours. Every finding this page calls correct or wrong, I checked against the manuscript myself. Treat the split between "full" and "partly" as a judgement call. Points 4, 7 and 8 were the close ones, and with looser grading both reviews would score about 9 or 10 of 12.

Two things stood out. First, the human reviewers were not exhaustive: both AI reviews found correct, material errors that none of the eLife reviewers raised. Second, the two AI reviews missed different things. Deep recomputes reported statistics in code, which is how it caught the one-tailed test, but it never checked the numbers in Table S2. The chat read every table closely but could not recompute anything. If you use one, the other is a reasonable second pass.

The tools, one at a time

Purplelink Paper Review

Seven AI reviewers read the manuscript, each from one angle (methods, statistics, data integrity, equations, literature, numbers, editor), and one pass reconciles what they found. Every reference is looked up in CrossRef, reported statistics are recomputed in code, figures are scanned, and the most important citations are checked against the cited abstract. You get a Markdown report and an annotated PDF. The same manuscript can then go through a Revision Review, which checks which findings you fixed, and a Response Review, which checks your response letter against the reviewers' comments. In this test it found more statistical errors than the chat and fewer table errors, and it made one high-confidence misdiagnosis. Your manuscript goes to Anthropic's API, which keeps inputs for up to 30 days and does not train on them; Purplelink deletes its copy within about a day. Refund within 14 days if the review is not useful. Best for a structured pass with recomputed statistics and an annotated PDF, followed by the revision and response steps on the same paper.

Plain ChatGPT or Claude

In this test a single careful prompt to Claude matched Deep on coverage and made fewer mistakes. It read the tables more closely. What it cannot do without tools is recompute statistics or look up references, and you get no fixed format, so a second run can come back different. Check the product's training settings before you paste unpublished work. ChatGPT was not tested here. Best for a free or already-paid first read, if you will check what it says against the paper.

Manusights

The closest direct competitor to Paper Review. The free Readiness Scan took about two minutes and returned a score of 68 out of 100 and one of its three top risks: that the paper's early-then-late brain-response claim rests on a single modest effect. That is a fair point and overlaps two of eLife's. It repeated the one-tailed p-value without noticing. The full review ($39) and a dossier with simulated reviewers ($99) were not bought. Its pages say uploads are not used for training and offer a 60-day money-back guarantee on the Full Review. Best for a free first look at your biggest risk, and journal-fit help if you pay.

Stanford PaperReview.ai

A free reviewer from a Stanford research group. It takes a PDF up to 10 MB, reads only the first 15 pages, and emails the review. It is tuned toward fields that post on arXiv, and the pages I read did not state a data policy. My result is pending and this entry will be updated. Best for a free second opinion on a short, computational paper.

Reviewer3 (not run)

Splits a paper into claims and checks the evidence for each, verifies references, looks for fatal flaws and can rerun submitted code. The free plan gives one full review with no card; Pro is $29 a month. Its security page says uploads are never used for training. Best for papers that ship code you want rerun against the text, and anyone with several papers a month.

q.e.d Science (not run)

Breaks a paper or grant into claims, marks gaps with suggested fixes and places the findings against related work. It is free for researchers with an institutional email. Its data statements conflict: the site says uploads are not used to train language models, while its terms allow it to train and evaluate its own models. Best for a free claims-and-gaps read, if those terms are acceptable for your manuscript.

Explore Science, formerly Paper-Wizard (not run)

A scorecard review with reference checks and a chat assistant, plus journal and funding matching. One full review is free after you verify an account; after that it is $50 a paper or a monthly plan. The site says the review takes hours. Best for one free full review when you are not in a hurry.

Thesify

Critiques argument, methods, evidence and structure, graded by severity, with journal suggestions. No free review was offered on the pricing page; one review is €14.95. It says documents are not used to train public models. Best for students and early-career authors who want feedback on the argument at a low one-off price.

Trinka (not run)

Mainly an academic grammar and language tool, with plagiarism, citation and "technical" checks bought as credits. The free Basic plan covers 5,000 words a month. Only the $300-a-year Confidential Data plan states no AI training. Best for language polishing, not methods critique.

Paperpal Preflight and Penelope.ai

Both check a manuscript against a journal's submission rules: word limits, required sections, declarations, reference style. Neither judges the science. Paperpal's pages did not show a price in the version I could read. Penelope.ai is sold to journals, from £750 a year, so most authors meet it at submission. Best for avoiding a formatting desk rejection.

SciScore

Checks a life-science methods section against rigor and reporting standards (blinding, randomisation, sample size, resource identifiers). Authors now start through Aseda Sciences with an ORCID sign-in; I did not find a current price. Best for wet-lab and animal studies, and little else.

No longer available or out of scope

StatReviewer, an automated statistics checker that ran inside journals' editorial systems, has no working site; the most recent material I found dates from 2016 to 2018. Enago's peer-review service page returned an error on November 13, 2026, so I have left its earlier price out. Writefull is a language tool and does not review a paper.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best AI paper reviewer?
It depends on the job. In my test on one eLife manuscript, Purplelink Paper Review's Deep tier and a careful plain prompt to Claude each raised all 12 of the human reviewers' points at least in part, and each found real errors the other missed. Paper Review recomputes reported statistics and gives a fixed report with an annotated PDF; the chat read tables more closely. Reviewer3 suits papers with code to rerun, Paperpal Preflight suits journal formatting, and SciScore suits life-science methods. None replaces a human reader who knows your field.
Can I just paste my paper into ChatGPT or Claude for a review?
Yes, and in my test it worked well: one plain prompt to Claude raised every eLife reviewer point at least in part and caught table errors our own tool missed. It cannot recompute statistics or look up references without tools, a second run can come back different, and it can state an error confidently. Check the product's training settings before you paste unpublished work.
Is there a free AI paper reviewer?
Several have a free route. Manusights gives a free Readiness Scan that shows one of its top risks. Reviewer3 gives one full review without a card. Explore Science gives one full review after you verify an account. q.e.d Science says it is free for researchers with an institutional email. Stanford's PaperReview.ai is free but reads only the first 15 pages. Paper Review has no free full review; its free step checks how many of your references CrossRef can confirm.
How much does an AI manuscript review cost?
From the vendors' own pages on November 13, 2026: Purplelink Paper Review is $14 Standard, $24 with a journal check and $39 Deep, with a $5 Revision Review and a $12 Response Review ($24 with a drafted letter). Manusights is $39 for a full review. Reviewer3 Pro is $29 a month. Thesify is €14.95 for one review. Explore Science is $50 a paper. Prices change, so check before you pay.
Did Paper Review make mistakes in the test?
Yes. It called a mislabelled odds-ratio column a high-confidence contradiction, read only 4 of the paper's references, and its automated check missed the paper's data and code statement. It also caught errors no other review found, including a one-tailed p-value reported as two-tailed. Read every finding against your paper, whichever tool you use.
What happens to my manuscript if I use Purplelink Paper Review?
It is sent to Anthropic's Claude API for analysis. Anthropic retains inputs for up to 30 days and does not train on them. Purplelink deletes the manuscript and the review from its own servers within about a day after you first open the result, or within 48 hours if you never do. Save the report before closing the tab.
Is an AI paper review a substitute for peer review?
No. In my test the AI reviews found real errors the human reviewers missed, and the human reviewers raised points about theory and design that the AI reviews only partly covered. Every AI review also said at least one thing that was wrong. Use one alongside at least one human reader who knows your area.
