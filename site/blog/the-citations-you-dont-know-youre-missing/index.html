The citations you don't know you're missing

By Benjamin Ampel · October 28, 2026

A reviewer's note that you missed a paper costs a full resubmission cycle. Here's why citation gaps happen, and how I built a tool to flag the related work your bibliography is missing before a reviewer does.

A reviewer who writes "the authors seem unaware of related work by X" is naming a citation gap: a paper your manuscript should cite and doesn't. That comment costs more time than almost any other note on a review. It surfaces after you've already submitted, when the fix is a resubmission cycle instead of a quick edit.

Citation gaps happen for a boring reason. You read deeply in your own subfield and thinly at its edges. The paper a reviewer expects you to know can sit just outside the search terms you used when the project started. Your related-work section gets written early and rarely revisited, while the literature keeps publishing. By the time you submit, the bibliography reflects the search you did two years ago, not the search a domain expert would run today.

Citation Gap Analysis is built for that exact blind spot. It runs in the opposite direction from a bibliography checker. Instead of confirming the citations you have are real, it reads your manuscript's claims against your existing bibliography and flags the papers you don't have but likely should. Each flagged item names the section where the citation belongs and explains why a reviewer would notice its absence. When the tool can identify a candidate paper, it names the author and title, checked against CrossRef so you know whether a record for it actually exists. A scan costs $3, takes about two minutes, and the full steps are in the guide to running one before you submit.

The tool's suggestions are AI recall, not certainty, so every one needs your own read before it goes in the bibliography. A title the model names with confidence can still be the wrong paper. It can also be close enough to the right one that citing it anyway looks careless to someone who knows the literature. The CrossRef check beside each suggestion exists for that reason: a confirmed record narrows your search, it doesn't finish it.

This is a narrower problem than most manuscript review covers. The free BibTeX Validator checks the references you already typed for broken DOIs and duplicate keys, a different failure mode entirely: a citation that's wrong, not one that's missing. Catching a citation your own drafting hallucinated is its own separate check too. Citation Gap sits between those two: before submission, after your related-work section feels finished, when you're confident enough in the bibliography that you've stopped questioning it.

I added this tool after watching the same comment land on manuscript after manuscript in Paper Review reports. Not a methods flaw, not a stats error. Just a missing name the field would expect to see. It's a cheap problem to fix before submission and an expensive one to fix during a revise-and-resubmit.

Further reading: your related-work section may read like a list of summaries, not an argument. They Say / I Say has the templates for agreeing, disagreeing, and saying why a cited paper matters to yours. The Craft of Research is the one I'd hand a student who needs to turn a topic into a question a reader will accept. That's most of what a related-work section is actually for. More books like these are on the research desk.
