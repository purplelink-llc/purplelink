## Option 1

This week I expanded the venue reference pages on purplelink.llc, the ones that explain how to format citations for ACM CCS, IEEE S&P, USENIX Security, and five other conferences and journals. Over the past 90 days the pages had about 191 impressions and 2 clicks. They ranked between positions 8 and 12 but were thin: 214 to 282 words each.

I added two sections: how to set up the bibliography in LaTeX, and a short list of things that commonly go wrong with that venue's format. Those sections exist for four of the eight venues, not all eight.

The four that are missing are deliberately empty. The bibliography setup section is only filled in where I can verify the exact .bst filename from the package itself. ACM-Reference-Format.bst ships inside acmart. IEEEtran.bst ships with IEEEtran. Both are checkable. Three venues ship a custom .bst whose filename appears only inside a ZIP template, and one venue uses Word entirely. For those venues, the section does not render.

A page that gives the right \bibliographystyle line is useful. A page that guesses and gets it wrong costs a researcher time they did not plan to spend. These are lines researchers copy directly into manuscripts before a deadline.

If there is a venue missing or a format detail that should be explained, I would be glad to add it.

## Option 2

AdSense rejected purplelink.llc in July for "low value content." I did not dispute it. I went to Search Console and looked at what the data actually showed.

Over 90 days: 54 auto-generated daily digest pages earned 13 impressions and 0 clicks. 73 hand-written pages earned 3,746 impressions and 54 clicks. The digest was 37% of the indexable site and produced 0.3% of its impressions.

The digest also accounts for about 49 of the site's 623 monthly pageviews, and those arrive by RSS, not search. Readers who wanted it were already subscribed.

This week I set all 54 daily issue pages to noindex. The RSS feed and topic hubs are untouched; the main /blog/digest/ landing page stays indexable. What changed is that machine-generated content is no longer being weighed against hand-written content in how a search engine classifies the site.

I had built the digest so researchers would have a reason to check back. The data said they were already doing that through RSS. Keeping the digest indexed because I had invested time in building it would have been the wrong reason to leave it.

If you run a site that mixes automated and hand-written content, I would be interested to know how you have handled that balance.

## Option 3

I added a 14-day traffic forecast to my internal dashboard this week. I want to explain the one thing I deliberately left out.

The standard feature for a forecast is a trend line: fit a regression, draw an arrow, see whether things are going up or down. I ran it on 21 days of data. The slope for Purplelink came out at -0.71 pageviews per day, t = -1.34. Residual noise was 67% of the signal level. Not distinguishable from flat.

So there is no trend line. The projection assumes the current daily rate holds and shows uncertainty as a band: 50% and 80% intervals from resampling observed days rather than from a normal distribution. A normal distribution produces negative values at the low end for these page counts, which is not a plausible outcome.

The chart note says: treat the band, not the centre line, as the finding. The centre line reverts to the 21-day mean, so this week the site reads as growing. That is mean reversion from a week that ran below average. Not a trend.

I build tools for researchers, so I try to hold my own metrics to the same standard I would hold a methods section. When the data cannot support a claim, the chart should not make it.

If you track traffic for a site or project and have found approaches that hold up at small sample sizes, I would be glad to hear what you use.

## Selected

Option 1 -- it is the most directly useful to the academic researcher audience (researchers use these pages when setting up LaTeX bibliographies before a submission deadline), the most specific (exact .bst filenames, exact word counts, real Search Console numbers), and the "deliberately empty" decision illustrates the craftsmanship and honesty brand most clearly.
