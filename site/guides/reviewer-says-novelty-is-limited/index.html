How to Respond When a Reviewer Says "Novelty Is Limited"

By Benjamin Ampel · July 3, 2026

This comment shows up in three different situations, and each needs a different response. The most common mistake is to treat all three the same way.

"Novelty is limited" is one of the most common reviewer comments across fields, and one of the least specific. Before drafting a response, work out which of three situations you are in. The diagnosis decides everything that follows.

Situation 1: the contribution is real but poorly framed

This is the most common case. The paper's introduction and related-work section describe what was done but never state what is different from the closest prior work. A reviewer reading quickly assumes the paper is incremental because nothing says otherwise.

Fix: Add a short, direct comparison (ideally a table) of your approach against the two or three closest prior works, naming exactly what is different in method, scope, or setting. In the response letter, state this comparison plainly: "Our work differs from [X] in that..." Do not just say the paper is novel. Show the specific difference.

Situation 2: the reviewer is not aware of a subfield distinction

Sometimes the reviewer has a different mental map of the literature than you do. They see your work as similar to a body of prior research. Specialists in your subfield understand that research to solve a different problem.

Fix: Respond respectfully and specifically, without implying the reviewer is uninformed. Name the prior works they are likely thinking of, and explain what distinguishes your problem setting, assumptions, or evaluation criteria from that line of work. Here, more citations to the specific works that distinguish yours help more than a general defense of novelty.

Situation 3: the contribution really is incremental

Sometimes the reviewer is right. If an honest look at the closest prior work shows a small step beyond what is already published, arguing novelty is a losing strategy. Reviewers who read closely will not be persuaded by assertion.

Fix: Reframe the paper instead of defending it. Many incremental contributions are still publishable when framed honestly: a careful replication, a negative result worth reporting, an application of an existing method to a new and non-trivial setting, or a useful dataset or benchmark. Adjust the paper's framing and, if needed, the target venue to match an incremental but useful contribution. Do not overclaim.

Writing the actual response

  • Open with the specific comparison, not a general defense: "Compared to [X] and [Y], our approach..." beats "We believe our work is novel because..."
  • Use a short comparison table if there are two or more closely related prior works. It is the fastest way for a reviewer to verify your claim.
  • Update the paper's related work section to match the response, not just the response letter. Future readers will not see your reply to reviewers.
  • If you are in situation 3, say so in the response and describe the reframing. Do not make a novelty argument you cannot fully defend.

If you want a second opinion before you argue novelty in a response letter, Response to Reviewers checks whether your draft addresses the reviewer's specific point or only asserts novelty without evidence. That gap is easy to miss when you are close to your own paper.
