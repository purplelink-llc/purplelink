Best Mac apps for transcription, text to speech and OCR (2026)

By Benjamin Ampel · October 9, 2026

Apps that turn speech into text, text into speech, and photos or scans into text, with the price of each, whether it runs on your Mac or in the cloud, and where it is the wrong choice.

Disclosure up front: Purplelink, which publishes this site, makes one app on this list, Tapefolio. I have kept its entry to what it does today, and I have not ranked it above anything. It is new, and where another app is the better choice I say so.

Prices checked on October 9, 2026, on each vendor's own page. I did not test the other apps. Prices change, and where a page did not say something I write "not stated" instead of guessing.

Start with where the file must stay

Ask one question before you compare prices: must the file stay on your Mac? Ethics boards (IRBs, institutional review boards), client contracts and confidentiality agreements can bar cloud tools for a given file, whatever they cost. In the tables, "cloud" means the file is sent to the vendor's servers. "On device" is the vendor's own statement; I have not audited any of them.

Match the app to the job:

  • The recording must stay on your Mac: MacWhisper, Aiko or Buzz, or Tapefolio below. Buzz is free and open source.
  • You edit transcripts like a document: Descript, which edits the audio by editing its text.
  • A person must check the words: Rev or Happy Scribe, which both sell human transcripts.
  • You transcribe a few hours a year: Sonix, pay as you go.

Every automatic transcript needs a listen-through. Whisper-based tools (the open speech model behind several apps above) can invent words, and their developers link this to pauses, background sounds and music [Gigazine summary]. A university library guide to running Whisper locally says the output may need review and editing, particularly with technical terms or several speakers [SMU Libraries]. Plan a correction pass, and quote only text you have checked against the audio.

Match the app to the job:

  • You want free and private: Apple Spoken Content. Turn on Speak selection in Read & Speak settings, select text and press Option-Escape. Piper adds more natural voices to the system, and you download them inside its app.
  • You read long PDFs in a dedicated reader: Speechify or ElevenReader. ElevenReader lists no Mac app.
  • You want no subscription: NaturalReader sells one-time licenses.
  • You want audio you can keep and edit locally: Voicebox, which takes more setup than a reader.

Match the app to the job:

  • You need one snippet from a screenshot: Live Text (Apple's built-in text recognition) or TextSniper.
  • You have one PDF with a poor text layer and no budget: macOCR, which runs OCR on every page and always writes PDF/A.
  • You have many scans: ask ABBYY or Adobe about batch support before you buy, since neither pricing page says, or use Tapefolio below.
  • You already pay for a PDF suite: Acrobat Pro lists turning scans into editable PDFs.

Google Drive can also convert a photo or PDF to text, but it uploads the file and, per Google's help page, the file should be 2 MB or smaller. Neither suits a confidential scan.

Where Tapefolio fits

Tapefolio is a Mac app for transcribing interviews and meetings and reading scans, made for people whose recordings cannot be uploaded. It costs $29.99 once after a 7-day trial. There is one download: after the trial it asks for a license key, and one key works on two Macs. It is also part of the $54.99 Mac Suite. It needs macOS 26 or later and a Mac with Apple silicon, with no Intel version. The current version is 1.0.1, released October 9, 2026, so it is the newest and least proven app here. As of that date it does the following:

  • Transcribes on your Mac with the time of every word, from audio files or from the microphone inside the app.
  • Speech model: the download is about 40 MB and includes no speech model. Until you download one, it uses Apple's on-device speech recognition, and macOS may fetch Apple's own model once. Better models are optional downloads in Settings, from Hugging Face, each once: NVIDIA Parakeet (English), Whisper base (English) and Whisper large-v3 turbo (many languages). Settings can delete any of them.
  • Labels speakers on your Mac. You name each speaker once and merge labels that are one person in the review screen, which plays the audio beside the text.
  • Remembers a voice only if you ask it to. Speaker memory is off until you turn it on for a project and record a consent note. Voices are stored encrypted on your Mac, the app only suggests matches for you to confirm, and you can delete them.
  • Replaces identifiers with codes: names, places, organizations, emails, phone numbers, addresses, links, ID numbers and dates of birth. You review each one, and the key file that maps the codes back is saved separately. It misses some, so you read the result.
  • Reads scans in batches: photos, scans and PDFs, one file or a folder, into a searchable PDF, text, Markdown, Word, JSON or CSV.
  • Exports transcripts to Word, HTML, Markdown, text, SRT, VTT and JSON, plus a timestamped Word file for MAXQDA.

What it does not do. It will mishear words and mislabel speakers. It does not anonymize a transcript or make a study compliant, and it cannot tell when a detail identifies someone only in context. It does not read text aloud; it has no text to speech. It has been measured on generated OCR test files only, not on handwriting or phone photos, and on English meeting audio only. It has no Intel, Windows, iPhone or iPad version.

The measurements, from its page. On two meetings from the AMI corpus, the word error rate was between 21% and 29% for Apple's engine, Parakeet and Whisper large-v3 turbo, and between 29% and 39% for Whisper base. Word accuracy on a real recorded two-person conversation has not been measured. OCR on nine generated test files (11 pages) had a word error rate of 1.5%. These are small checks on one machine, so treat every transcript as a draft.

Transcription, speaker labels, identifier removal and OCR run on the Mac, and nothing from your files is uploaded. The internet is used for the optional model downloads, an update check, and macOS itself if you use Apple's speech recognition. Whether that meets your ethics board's rules is for you and the board to decide.

If a free tool above does the job, use that. Buzz or MacWhisper's free tier handle a transcript that must stay on your Mac, and Live Text handles a single image. Tapefolio is for the case where you want speaker labels, identifier replacement, a review screen and batch OCR in one place, and you have a Mac that can run it.

Choose in one minute

  1. Decide whether the file must stay on your Mac, and if it must, consider on-device apps only.
  2. Count the files, and use the built-in tools for one file and a batch tool for hundreds.
  3. Decide who will rely on the exact words, and if anyone will, pay for a human check or listen back yourself.

Frequently asked

Which Mac transcription apps keep the recording on my Mac?

MacWhisper, Aiko, Buzz and Tapefolio (which I make) say they transcribe on the device. MacWhisper can also be pointed at cloud models with your own API keys, so check its settings. Otter, Descript, Happy Scribe, Sonix and Rev are cloud services. Whether an on-device app meets your ethics board's rules is for you and the board to decide.

Is there a free way to read text aloud on a Mac?

Yes. Turn on Speak selection in Read & Speak settings, select text and press Option-Escape. Piper - Neural TTS is a free App Store app that adds more natural voices to the system, and Voicebox is free and open source.

Does Tapefolio read text aloud?

No. Tapefolio transcribes speech and reads scans into text. It has no text to speech. For that, use one of the apps in the text to speech table.

Can OCR on a Mac be done without uploading the file?

Yes. Live Text in Preview and TextSniper work on a single image, macOCR handles one PDF at a time, and Tapefolio reads a whole folder, all on the Mac. Google Drive's image-to-text conversion uploads the file.
