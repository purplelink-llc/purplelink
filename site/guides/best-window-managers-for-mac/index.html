Best window managers for Mac (2026): Rectangle, Magnet, Moom and Panely compared

By Benjamin Ampel · October 12, 2026, updated October 27, 2026

Apps and built-in tools that snap and arrange windows on a Mac. The short version: macOS covers halves and quarters for free, Rectangle is the usual free pick, and Magnet and BetterSnapTool are low-priced. Moom is the deepest, and AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst tile automatically. The real differences are custom layouts, what happens when you reconnect a monitor, price model and permissions.

Disclosure: Purplelink, which publishes this site, makes one app on this list, Panely, sold as a free 7-day trial and then $9.99 once. I write about it from its own product page, and where another app is the better choice I say so.

I did not install or test any of the other apps. Everything below is read from Apple's help pages and from each vendor's own page, store listing or repository, on October 12, 2026. Prices, versions and features change. "Not stated" means the page I read did not say. I left out any claim I could not find on a vendor or Apple page.

The short answer

Try what is already in macOS first. It snaps windows to halves, quarters and more by dragging or with Fn-Control shortcuts, and it costs nothing. If you want more, the common free pick is Rectangle, the low-priced App Store picks are Magnet and BetterSnapTool, and Moom is the deepest for custom layouts. AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst are a different kind of tool that tiles automatically. Panely, which I make, is for snapping plus saved layouts that return after a monitor is reconnected, for a single $9.99 payment.

What window managers do, and what they do not

Two different ideas share the name "window manager", and most of the confusion in comparisons comes from mixing them.

  1. Snappers. You ask for a place: a shortcut, a drag to an edge, a menu. The window goes there and stays until you move it. macOS, Rectangle, Magnet, BetterSnapTool, Moom, Loop, Swish and Panely work this way.
  2. Automatic tiling managers. The app decides the arrangement and re-tiles as windows open and close. AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst work this way. They suit people who like to configure a system and keep their hands on the keyboard. They also ask you to learn a model.

Whichever you pick, a window manager needs the Accessibility permission, which is how macOS lets an app move other apps' windows. You can check or switch it off in System Settings, Privacy & Security, Accessibility. No manager can resize a window below the minimum its app allows.

The shortlist

  1. macOS built in: free, nothing to install, fixed layouts.
  2. Rectangle: free and open source, with a paid Pro.
  3. Magnet: $4.99 on the Mac App Store.
  4. BetterSnapTool: custom snap areas, $1.99 on its listing.
  5. Moom: $15, custom actions and saved layouts.
  6. Loop: free, a radial menu.
  7. Swish: trackpad gestures.
  8. Lasso: a grid you drag across, with saved layouts.
  9. Mosaic: drag-to-place and custom layouts; unclear support for the newest macOS.
  10. AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst: automatic tiling.
  11. Panely: mine, $9.99 once, with saved layouts.

macOS built in

Cost: free. Platform: Apple's help describes window tiling for macOS Sequoia 15 and later, with pages for Sequoia, Tahoe 26 and macOS 27.

Apple lists five ways to tile. You can drag a window to an edge, hold Option while dragging to a highlighted area, use the green button's menu, use the Window menu under Move & Resize, or use keyboard shortcuts. Dragging to the menu bar fills the desktop. Shortcuts on Apple's list include Fn-Control-F to fill, Fn-Control-C to center, Fn-Control with an arrow key for a half, and Fn-Control-R to return to the previous size. Adding Shift arranges two windows side by side or stacked. Corner quarters have no shortcut on that list.

Settings are under System Settings, Desktop & Dock, Windows, and include whether dragging to an edge tiles and whether tiled windows have margins. Apple's page words them as "Drag windows to left or right edge of screen to tile" and "Tiled windows have margins". Names can differ between macOS versions.

What I did not find: custom areas, saved layouts, or anything about remembering windows across monitors on the pages I read. Pick it if halves and quarters are all you need. See also how to snap and tile windows on a Mac.

Rectangle

Cost: free and open source. Its site also offers Rectangle Pro, free for 10 days, and its docs describe the license as a one-time, lifetime purchase usable on three devices at once. I could not read the Pro price on those pages. Platform: the repository says macOS 14 and later, with older releases for older systems.

Rectangle is the app most people mean by "a free window manager". Keyboard shortcuts move and resize windows, and repeating a shortcut cycles through sizes. Dragging to an edge or corner snaps to halves, quarters, thirds or maximized, and snapping can be turned off. It has next and previous display actions, a restore action, extra shortcuts for more positions, per-app shortcut disabling, and JSON import and export of settings. Pro adds custom snap areas and custom sizes. It also arranges a set of apps with one shortcut (optionally when displays connect or disconnect), stashes windows at an edge, and syncs through iCloud, per its Pro page.

Where it beats Panely: it is free and open source, supports older macOS releases, and has been the default recommendation for years. Pick it if you want a capable free snapper. The pages I read describe no saved arrangements in the free app. Rectangle Pro and Panely cover that gap.

Magnet

Cost: $4.99 on the Mac App Store, which describes it as a perpetual license with no subscription or in-app purchases. Platform: macOS 13.0 or later, by BOOTCODE A.S.

Per Magnet's site, you activate it by dragging, customizable keyboard shortcuts or the menu bar. It snaps to halves, quarters and thirds, supports up to six external displays, and adapts to vertical screens. The listing adds optional iCloud sync and unlimited custom commands. The developer reports on the listing that it collects no data.

What I did not find: a restore action or saved layouts on those pages. Pick it if you want an established paid snapper from the App Store.

BetterSnapTool

Cost: $1.99 with in-app purchases on the Mac App Store listing. The vendor page links to a separate license and gives no price I could read. Platform: macOS 12.0 or later, by folivora.AI GmbH.

The listing describes drag to an edge or corner, halves and quarters, custom snap areas, multiple monitors, customizable shortcuts, and moving or resizing with modifier keys. The vendor page lists custom snap areas anywhere on the display.

What I did not find: saved arrangements. Pick it if you want the lowest entry price and custom snap areas. It also supports an older macOS than most.

Moom

Cost: $15, with a free trial, per Many Tricks. The page says the license is valid forever and includes updates for at least one year. Platform: Moom 4.6 requires macOS 10.13 or newer.

Moom is the long-running power tool. It has a pop-up palette of up to 61 custom actions that can be chained. It also has an on-screen grid, a keyboard controller, snap zones, and hover-and-modifier moving. Layouts can be saved per app or for the most recently used windows. Saved layouts try to adjust to displays of other sizes. The page says this suits laptops that sometimes use an external display.

Where it beats Panely: depth of custom actions and a long history. Pick it if you want to script your own window moves and do not mind a higher price and a busier interface.

Loop

Cost: free and GPLv3-licensed, per its repository, which also has a sponsor link. Platform: the README says macOS 13 and later. However, a table on the same page says macOS 15 or later, so check before installing.

You hold a trigger key and move the cursor over a radial menu to choose where a window goes. A preview appears before you commit. Keys can be paired with the trigger, repeated combinations cycle actions, windows can be stashed at the screen edge, and a URL scheme allows scripting.

Pick it if you like a radial menu and open source. I did not find drag to edges or multi-display behavior stated on that page.

Swish

Cost: $16 once with a 14-day trial, per its site, which also says it is in Setapp. One license works on two Macs. Platform: macOS 12 or later.

Swish is gesture based. It offers about 30 trackpad gestures for windows, the Dock and the menu bar. You snap by swiping a title bar to 2x2, 3x2 or 3x3 grids, resize several windows at once by dragging a divider, and move windows between screens. It also takes keyboard shortcuts.

Pick it if you work on a trackpad and want gestures rather than shortcuts. Swish is also available on Setapp(paid link).

Lasso

Cost: Setapp lists Lasso on its own from $6.99 a month, and the developer's site offers a 7-day trial and a license sold through Paddle. I did not find a direct price on the pages I read; a Macworld review of an older version listed 9.99 euros for one Mac. Platform: macOS 13 or later. Setapp lists version 1.8.2.

You press a hotkey, a transparent grid appears over your displays, and you drag across it to choose where a window goes. The Setapp page also lists saved layouts with their own hotkeys, adjustable grids and spacing, multi-display support and iCloud settings sync.

Limits: it is a tool you drive, so it does not tile for you. Two Setapp reviews are worth knowing: one asks whether it crashes on macOS 27, and one found it hard to learn. I found no dated release notes.

Pick it if you like placing a window by drawing its area rather than remembering shortcuts. Lasso is also available on Setapp(paid link). Based on the Setapp listing, the developer's site, a Macworld review and user reviews; we have not tested it ourselves yet.

Mosaic

Cost: Setapp lists Mosaic alone at $5.99 a month. I did not find a standalone price on the pages I read. Platform: Setapp lists version 1.5.1 for macOS 10.13 or later.

Mosaic, from Light Pillar Software, snaps and resizes windows by drag and drop, tiles several apps or documents, saves custom layouts, applies a layout across displays, and has Touch Bar support and an iOS remote. Setapp shows 92 percent positive ratings from about 2,600 ratings.

Limits: I could not confirm when it last shipped an update or whether it supports the newest macOS. A 2025 Setapp review says the latest macOS was not supported, while 2026 reviews of 1.5.1 are positive, and a couple of reviewers call it dated or hard to figure out. Check the developer's page for your macOS version before paying.

Pick it if you want custom layouts and drag placement and your macOS version is confirmed. Mosaic is also available on Setapp(paid link). Based on the Setapp listing and user reviews, plus a 2017 MacTrast review of the first version; we have not tested it ourselves yet.

AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst

These are tiling window managers. You describe rules, and the app arranges everything. The approach differs from the snappers above, and the cost is a learning curve.

AeroSpace is, per its guide, an i3-like tiling manager for Apple silicon and Intel, configured in a TOML file, with a CLI (command-line interface). It reimplements Spaces as its own "workspaces" and suggests using a single macOS Space after that. Its README says it runs on macOS 13 or later and that it will never require turning off System Integrity Protection (SIP).

yabai is, per its repository, an MIT-licensed tiling manager based on binary space partitioning. It is controlled from the command line, with shortcuts from skhd or other software. It requires Accessibility and needs Screen Recording only for window animations. Some features need System Integrity Protection partly disabled for a scripting addition. The page lists support from macOS Big Sur on Intel and Monterey on Apple silicon through Tahoe 26.

Amethyst is, per its repository, a free, MIT-licensed tiling manager "à la xmonad" for macOS 10.15 and later. Its layouts include Tall, Wide, Two Pane, three-column, Fullscreen and BSP, and it has a beta custom layout in JavaScript. It needs the Accessibility permission.

Pick one of these if you want windows tiled for you. If you only want to place a window when you ask, they are more machinery than you need.

Panely (Purplelink's)

Cost: free for 7 days, then $9.99 once, every update included. One key works on two Macs. Panely is also part of the $59.99 Mac Suite. Platform: macOS 14 or later, a direct download from its page, with no Mac App Store build. I make it, it is new, and I have not compared it with the others on a test Mac.

Panely snaps the front window to halves, thirds, quarters, two thirds and sixths. It also offers maximize, almost maximize, center and a step larger or smaller. You trigger these from a shortcut, the menu bar, or a drag to an edge or corner, with a preview of where the window will land. Every shortcut can be re-recorded, reset or turned off, and Panely refuses combinations macOS keeps for itself. A margin and a gap between windows run from 0 to 32 points.

Beyond snapping, Panely offers four more features. First, up to twelve custom areas, set as percentages of the screen so they fit any display. Second, saved layouts of your open windows that you apply from the menu bar. Third, a layout you describe in words, read by fixed rules on your Mac rather than a model. Fourth, an automatic layout for each arrangement of displays that puts windows back a few seconds after a display is plugged in again. It can ignore chosen apps, such as a game or a video call, and it restores a window to its size before the first snap.

It needs the Accessibility permission, reads each window's position, size and title and nothing inside it, and works offline. It uses macOS's own global shortcut system, so it does not need Input Monitoring. Its only network use is its check for a new version.

What it lacks: it is Mac only, new, and macOS 14 or later, which is a higher floor than Magnet, BetterSnapTool, Moom or Swish. It is not open source. It does not tile automatically. It works only on windows in the current Space, because macOS gives apps no way to move windows between Spaces.

Where others are better today: Rectangle for free and open source, Magnet or BetterSnapTool for a lower price, Moom for custom actions, AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst for automatic tiling. Pick Panely if you want snapping and a saved arrangement that comes back when you reconnect a monitor, with one payment and no subscription.

Quick picks

  • Only halves and quarters? macOS built in.
  • Want free and open source? Rectangle, or Loop for a radial menu.
  • Want the lowest paid price? BetterSnapTool or Magnet from the App Store.
  • Want custom actions and layouts? Moom, or Panely for saved layouts with a one-time price.
  • Want to restore windows after a monitor change? Panely, Moom's saved layouts, or Rectangle Pro.
  • Want windows tiled for you? AeroSpace, yabai or Amethyst.
  • Want trackpad gestures? Swish.
  • Want to draw each window's area on a grid? Lasso.

How to choose

  1. Decide whether you want to place windows or have them placed. Snappers act when you ask. Tiling managers rearrange by themselves.
  2. Start with the built-in tools. If halves and quarters cover you, you may not need an app. The how-to guide shows the steps.
  3. Count your displays. If you plug a laptop into a monitor and back, look for saved layouts or a per-monitor memory, not just snapping.
  4. Check the price model. Free, one-time or paid upgrade. A license that covers a second Mac matters if you have two.
  5. Check your macOS version. Floors run from macOS 10.13 for Moom to macOS 14 for Panely and the current Rectangle.
  6. Read the permission. Window managers use Accessibility. A repository you can read is an advantage for a tool with that access.
  7. Turn off overlapping behavior. If you add an app and keep macOS's drag-to-edge tiling on, a drag may snap twice.

Frequently asked

What is the best window manager for Mac?

There is no single best one. macOS's built-in tiling covers halves and quarters for free. Rectangle is the usual free, open source pick. Magnet and BetterSnapTool are low-priced App Store options, Moom is the deepest for custom actions, and AeroSpace, yabai and Amethyst tile automatically. Panely, which I make, is for snapping plus saved layouts. I tested none of the others, so this is a reading of vendor pages, not a ranking by use.

Does macOS have built-in window snapping?

Yes. Apple's help describes tiling in macOS Sequoia 15 and later. You can drag a window to an edge, hold Option while dragging, use the green button menu or the Window menu under Move & Resize, or use Fn-Control shortcuts. It offers fixed layouts and no custom areas on the pages I read.

Is Rectangle free?

Rectangle itself is free and open source. Rectangle Pro is a separate paid option with a 10-day trial. Its docs describe a one-time, lifetime license usable on three devices at once. I could not read the Pro price on those pages.

What is the difference between a window manager and a tiling window manager?

A snapper such as Rectangle, Magnet or Panely moves a window when you ask. A tiling manager such as AeroSpace, yabai or Amethyst arranges all windows by rule and re-tiles as windows open and close. Tiling managers take more setup.

Do window managers need special permissions?

They use the macOS Accessibility permission to move other apps' windows. yabai also lists Screen Recording for animations, and some yabai features need System Integrity Protection partly disabled. AeroSpace says it will never require that. Panely uses Accessibility and does not need Input Monitoring; Screen Recording is optional, only for pictures of windows in its fill-the-rest list.

Will a window manager restore my layout after I reconnect a monitor?

Some can. Panely keeps an automatic layout for each arrangement of displays. Moom saves layouts that adjust to other display sizes. Rectangle Pro can arrange a set of apps when displays connect or disconnect. Magnet and the free Rectangle do not state this on the pages I read.

Is Panely available, and what does it cost?

Panely is Purplelink's Mac menu-bar window manager: free for 7 days, then $9.99 once, for macOS 14 or later. I make it, it is new, and this guide describes it from its own product page rather than from a test against the others.
