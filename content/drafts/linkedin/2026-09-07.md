## Option 1

On September 5, four ModernTex buyers did not receive a download email. Sales completed, files downloaded, and nothing looked wrong from the outside. The bug ran for two days before I found it.

The cause was a signing secret mismatch. A Stripe webhook endpoint has its own secret -- different from the API key -- and I had installed the wrong site's secret in the environment. Netlify received every webhook delivery and rejected all of them with a 400 invalid_signature error, but logged zero invocations for those calls. That discrepancy cost an hour of the wrong investigation.

The tricky part is that the failure was invisible in the places I was checking. ModernTex uses a session-gated download URL that never talks to the webhook, so files actually reached buyers. Only the follow-up email was lost. My alert function also lives inside the handler, after the signature check, so a rejected delivery cannot trigger an alert. The error state produced output that looked like success.

I documented this in the runbook: where the actual delivery count lives (Stripe's Event deliveries view, per endpoint, not the Netlify function logs), how to test a fix without emailing real customers, and what the retry backlog will do afterward.

If you have run into webhook configuration bugs that produced correct-looking output, I would be curious what finally made the problem visible.

## Option 2

GlobePin 1.0 is on the App Store. I shipped it on September 3, then updated the product page on September 6 -- three days later. The page was still showing "In development" and a waitlist signup that had never taken a single response.

I spent time building that waitlist flow. Nobody signed up. The app launched, and I had to go retire the infrastructure I had built to collect signups that did not exist. No list, no emails to send, nothing to do except update the page copy and remove the form.

The lesson I keep relearning: a waitlist makes sense when there is meaningful uncertainty about whether people want a thing. For a free travel-tracking app on the App Store, the waitlist just delayed getting the download link in front of people who would have clicked it.

What shipped: GlobePin is a free iOS app for tracking places visited, flights taken, and destinations on a list. Map views on a 3D globe, flight statistics, and shareable travel postcards. iCloud sync, no account required. The App Store listing ID is 6762313540, if you want to verify the store page before downloading.

If you use any iOS travel tracking app, I would be curious what keeps the data entry habit from fading after the first few trips.

## Option 3

When I priced ModernTex at ten dollars, I also had to decide how to hand the file to someone who just paid. The two approaches have different failure modes.

The first is webhook-dependent delivery: Stripe fires an event, your server receives it, writes an entitlement to a database, and the buyer redeems that on the success page. If the webhook fails silently -- wrong signing secret, misconfigured endpoint -- the buyer pays, lands on the success page, and gets an error with no way to retrieve what they bought.

The second is session-gated delivery: the success URL carries the Stripe checkout session ID. The server verifies the session directly with Stripe at redemption time. No intermediate state, no webhook dependency.

ModernTex uses the second approach. The webhook still fires and still sends a confirmation email, but the download does not depend on it. When the webhook failed last week -- wrong signing secret, all deliveries silently rejected -- four buyers never received that email. The download still worked. They did not know anything had broken.

I made that tradeoff deliberately. A product that cannot be delivered becomes a refund request immediately. A product that delivers without a confirmation email is a fixable problem you find out about later.

For Paper Review, the webhook is load-bearing: the review entitlement is written there. That tool does not survive a signing secret mismatch the way ModernTex does. I designed it that way before I understood how easy the mismatch is to make.

If you have built digital delivery, I would be curious which failure mode you thought about first.

## Selected

Option 3 -- it is the most concrete and specific, names a real design decision with clear reasoning, connects honestly to a real incident this week, and shows Purplelink's design thinking in a way the other two do not. Option 1 is good but reads more as an incident report. Option 2 is honest but the product (a free travel app) is less directly relevant to the core audience of academic researchers and Apple-platform professionals.
