## Option 1

This week I turned on something I had been testing behind a flag for about three weeks: a coherence check inside Paper Review.

The paid review already asks whether a paper is good. This asks a narrower question, the one a reviewer answers in the first hour and an author almost never catches in their own draft: does the paper agree with itself. A hypothesis named in the theory section but never reported in results. An abstract that still says 87.4 percent after the number in the results table changed to 84.7. A construct defined as a belief on page 4 and treated as a behavior on page 19.

The part I spent the most time on was not finding these, it was proving they are real. The check builds a short summary of what each section of the paper claims, and every quote it shows you gets matched against the actual manuscript text before it is allowed into the report. If a quote is not verbatim, the finding gets dropped, even when the underlying issue is probably real.

It is live now in every Standard, Journal Pack and Deep Review report, at no extra cost: https://purplelink.llc/tools/paper-review/.

## Option 2

This week I went back through the free tools on the site and fixed something that was quietly wrong.

Several pages, including LaTeX Diff, said files were "never written to disk." That was not true. LaTeX Diff has to write both versions of your manuscript to a temporary folder on the server so latexdiff can compare them and the result can compile. The files are deleted right after, but they do exist on disk for those few seconds, and the old wording said otherwise.

I went through every free tool making a similar claim, LaTeX Diff, the Markdown to PDF converter, the BibTeX Builder and Validator, and the compile tools, and rewrote each one to say what actually happens: files sit in a temporary folder only while the job runs, then they are deleted. Nothing changed about how the tools work. What changed is that the words on the page now match the code behind them.

Nobody wrote in about this. I found it rereading my own copy against my own code, which is a slower way to catch a mistake than having someone tell you, but it is the only way I actually trust.

The tools are at https://purplelink.llc/tools/.

## Option 3

ModernTex hit version 2.0.0 this week, the thirty-second update since it launched on September 4.

The bump is not a rewrite. It is a split editor so you can see two parts of a manuscript at once, multiple cursors, project-wide find, Vim mode for people who have been editing text in Vim since before LaTeX had a GUI at all, and a visual equation editor so you can build a matrix or a piecewise function without memorizing the command for it. Underneath that: HTML and EPUB export, knitr and PythonTeX support, Zotero, a built-in terminal, remote projects, macros, plugins, custom themes, and a German interface in beta.

The version number is mostly an honest label. 1.11 had quietly become a different app than 1.0, and calling this one 2.0 is more accurate than pretending it is still the same app with a few patches on top. Most of what shipped this week came from requests I got from people actually using it, not a roadmap I wrote alone months ago.

It is free to try for seven days, then $19.99 once, no subscription: https://purplelink.llc/moderntex/.

I would like to hear which one of these you would have asked for first, or what is still missing from your own LaTeX workflow.

## Selected
Option 1 -- it is the most concrete and honest of the three: a real engineering decision (dropping a finding rather than let an unverified quote through) made in service of a real researcher problem (a manuscript that contradicts itself), shipped this week at no added cost, and it speaks directly to the academic-researcher audience rather than only Mac users.
