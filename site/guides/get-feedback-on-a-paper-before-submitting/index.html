How to Get Feedback on a Paper Before Submitting: Options Compared

By Benjamin Ampel · September 24, 2026

Colleagues, reading groups, writing centers, editing services, AI chat assistants, preprints and AI paper review tools compared on cost, turnaround, what each catches and what it misses.

The short answer: get one read from a person who knows your field, for framing and contribution, and one systematic check of methods, statistics, references, figures and formatting, which a structured tool or a paid service can do faster than a busy colleague.

No single option covers everything a journal reviewer will look at. A trusted colleague is the best judge of whether your contribution matters, and the worst person to ask to check 60 DOIs. An AI tool will check every reference in minutes and still miss that your framing ignores the debate your subfield has been having for three years. Below: what a pre-submission read should cover, how each option does, and a sequence that uses each for what it is good at.

I wrote this as the person who builds Paper Review, one of the options below. I have tried to describe it with the same plainness as the others, including what it does not do.

What a pre-submission read should check

Whoever reads your draft, these are the things reviewers and editors most often raise. Use the list to decide which reader covers which item.

  • Methods. Is the design able to answer the question you pose? Are baselines, controls and comparison conditions fair? Could someone replicate the procedure from what is written?
  • Statistics. Is the sample size justified? Are multiple comparisons handled? Are effect sizes and uncertainty reported, not only p-values? The Why it matters series covers the common failures one by one.
  • Claim-evidence alignment. Does the abstract say what the results tables show? "Outperforms" and "significantly" are the words that most often outrun the data.
  • References that resolve. Every DOI should point to a real paper with the title you cite, and the cited source should say what you say it says. This matters more now that AI-drafted text can introduce plausible citations that do not exist.
  • Figures. Readable axes, error bars where they belong, no truncated or broken scales that exaggerate a gap, and captions that match the plotted data.
  • Anonymization. For double-blind venues: no author names, institutions, grant numbers, ethics protocol numbers, author-owned URLs or "in our previous work" self-citations left in the body.
  • Journal compliance. Word and figure limits, required sections, data availability and reporting statements, reference style.
  • Cover letter. A short, plain statement of what the paper contributes and why it fits this journal.

Where Paper Review fits, and where it doesn't

Paper Review is built for the systematic part of the checklist: the parts that are tedious for a person and easy to skip under deadline. You upload a PDF (up to 20 MB) and pick one of five domain profiles. A vision pass looks at every page for figure problems such as broken axes or missing error bars. Every reference is checked against CrossRef, and for the most load-bearing claims the cited source's abstract is fetched and compared to what you wrote. Four personas (Methodology Critic, Statistical Skeptic, Data Integrity Officer and Editor-in-Chief) read the paper in parallel, and findings raised independently by more than one of them are listed first. You get a Markdown report with an A/B/C rectification checklist, plus an annotated PDF with comments placed on the pages they refer to.

What it does not do matters as much. It will not tell you, the way a senior colleague can, that your framing misreads where the field is heading. It reads methods as written, so if the description is accurate and the analysis is wrong in code, it will not see that. Its citation check works at the abstract level, so a claim supported only in a paper's full text is marked "not supported by abstract" rather than wrong, and you have to check. Some findings will be mistaken. Each one quotes the passage it is about, so you can confirm or dismiss it quickly. The review is advisory; every decision stays with you. If a review reads as low quality, email within 14 days of purchase and the price is refunded.

A workable sequence

For a paper you care about, spread the reads out so each one catches what the others miss.

  1. Four to six weeks out: a person who knows the field. Send your advisor, a co-author or a trusted colleague the draft with a specific question: "Is the contribution clear in the first two pages?" or "Is this the right venue?" Framing problems take the longest to fix, so find them first. A reading group session works well here too.
  2. Once the draft is close to final: a systematic check. Run Paper Review, with the Journal Pack if your target journal is supported. Work through the A items first. The walkthrough guide explains how to read each section of the report. If you only want a reference existence check, the free BibTeX Validator covers that part.
  3. If reviewers in your area are quick to say "you missed prior work": run Citation Gap Analysis ($3), which suggests papers an expert reviewer would expect you to cite. Treat each suggestion as a candidate to verify, since AI recall can be wrong.
  4. For double-blind venues, on the exact file you will upload: run Anonymity Check ($2). Paper Review includes an anonymity scan, but edits made after the review can reintroduce a name or a grant number, so check the final version. Still inspect figures, supplementary files and PDF metadata by hand. The double-blind guide lists what to strip.
  5. Last: the cover letter. Write it yourself or start from a draft with Cover Letter ($2), which works from your abstract and the journal name and never receives the full manuscript. See the cover letter guide.

When reviews come back, the same logic applies in reverse. How to respond to Reviewer 2 covers the reply, and Response to Reviewers ($6) checks whether your response letter addresses every comment and whether the revised manuscript contains the fixes you claim.

Q&A

When should I ask for feedback on a paper before submitting?

Ask a colleague or advisor for a read on framing and contribution four to six weeks before your target date, while there is still time to restructure. Run systematic checks on methods, statistics, references and formatting once the draft is close to final, and run a blinding check on the exact file you will upload.

Can I paste my manuscript into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini for a review?

You can, and it will often produce useful comments on clarity and argument. It is less reliable for systematic work: long manuscripts can be read unevenly, references are not checked against a bibliographic database unless the assistant has a lookup tool and uses it for every entry, and whether your text may be used for model training depends on the product, the plan and your settings. Check those settings before pasting unpublished work.

Is an AI paper review tool a substitute for a domain expert?

No. An AI review can check a manuscript systematically and quickly, but it cannot tell you with an expert's confidence whether the contribution matters to your subfield, and it can be wrong. Use it alongside at least one human reader who knows the area.

What happens to my manuscript if I use Purplelink Paper Review?

The manuscript is sent to Anthropic's Claude API for analysis. Anthropic retains inputs for up to 30 days for abuse monitoring and does not use them for model training. Purplelink deletes the manuscript and the review from its servers when you retrieve the result, so save the report before closing the tab. Redact identifiable patient data before uploading.

Does posting a preprint count as getting feedback?

Sometimes. A preprint on arXiv, SSRN or OSF can attract comments, but many preprints receive few or none, and the manuscript becomes public. Check the target journal's preprint policy first, and for double-blind venues check whether a public preprint is allowed and how it should be handled.
